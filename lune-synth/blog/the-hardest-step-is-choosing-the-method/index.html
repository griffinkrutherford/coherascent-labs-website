Learning Science August 31, 2026 By Griffin Rutherford

The hardest step is choosing the method

A worksheet can tell you which formula to use before you read the problem. Mixed practice puts that decision back in your hands.

The heading says “The quadratic formula.” Beneath it are twelve quadratic equations. You work through them, check your answers, and get most of them right. Then a test mixes equations, graphs, and word problems. Suddenly, getting started is the hard part.

The worksheet supplied something the test took away: the decision about which method belongs here. You practiced carrying out a procedure. The test also asked you to select it.

The heading is a hint

Grouped practice has a useful job. When a method is new, several similar problems give you room to understand its steps without making every decision at once. A worked example followed by a few attempts can help you get oriented.

But a long run of the same problem type can conceal a gap. You may be able to complete the steps while still being unsure what features make those steps appropriate. The chapter title is doing the classification for you.

Take a small example: x² = 25 and x² + 5x = 0. Both contain a squared term. For the first, taking square roots is a direct route. For the second, factoring out x makes the zero-product rule available. Being able to describe that difference is part of being able to solve the problems.

Give yourself the selection task

Interleaving means mixing different kinds of practice instead of finishing one kind before starting the next. In a 2010 experiment, Kelli Taylor and Doug Rohrer had children practice four types of mathematics problems while holding spacing constant. Mixed practice produced worse performance during practice but better performance on the next day's test. Their error analysis suggested a benefit in matching problems to procedures. Read the study.

That is evidence from a particular task, not a promise that shuffling every assignment improves every outcome. A useful application is to mix a few related methods once you have enough instruction to attempt each one.

Before you calculate, name the clue that made you choose the method.

Try a six-problem shuffle

  1. Choose three problem types you have already been taught. Pick two examples of each.
  2. Put them in a mixed order and cover headings that reveal the method.
  3. Before solving each one, write a short reason for your first move: “There is a common factor,” or “I need the rate of change.”
  4. Check both your method choice and your execution against a reliable solution.
  5. For a wrong choice, compare that problem with one where your chosen method would have fit. Name the difference.

If you cannot start any of the six, reduce the scope. Return to an explanation or worked example, then try distinguishing just two types. Randomness alone is not an instructional plan.

Ask for a clue before a procedure

This also changes how you ask for help. “Which formula do I use?” lets a tutor complete the selection step. “What feature of this problem should I pay attention to?” can leave you with a decision to make.

When using an AI tutor, write down your proposed method and reason first. Ask it to check whether the reason fits. Verify questionable advice against your course materials, and keep the final attempt in your own words and steps.

That is a useful standard for the work we want to support in Lune Synth: a solution should show why the first move belongs, as well as whether the arithmetic that follows is correct.
