The story behind Lune Synth

Learning worth
being present for.

I’m Griffin Rutherford, founder of Lune Synth. I built it because I wanted technology to help me do the work that makes life feel meaningful.

That means slowing down, facing a difficult idea, and staying with it until the pieces begin to fit.

Why I built it ↓

A note from Griffin

I wanted my attention back.

I got fed up with the attention economy, AI slop, and limbic capitalism: a business model built around pulling at our impulses. I felt those forces aligning against my ability to be present and do deep work. There was always something easier to click, consume, or hand off.

I’ve struggled with this too. AI slop and the attention economy have pulled me away from the things I actually wanted to be doing. I call that state “anti-presence”: being occupied without feeling fully engaged, letting the next distraction or effortless output take the place of deliberate attention. It is the antithesis of Lune Synth’s vision.

But the times I feel most fulfilled and proud of myself look very different. I’m fully present, facing one task, somewhere in the balance between ease and friction. There is enough challenge to demand my attention, and enough traction to keep going. That is the flow state I want more of in my life.

I’m an avid outdoor recreationist and a strength and endurance athlete. Effort is a familiar part of the things I love. I carry that same appreciation into learning: the work can be demanding, and the accomplishment can be deeply rewarding.

From gibberish to understanding.

I spent a huge part of my life as a student. At Colorado School of Mines, I earned both a B.S. and an M.S. in Computer Science, with a focus on artificial intelligence, data science, and machine learning. My work has since taken me into applied AI, transformer architectures, and autonomous agents. More about my background and projects.

Engineering school gave me plenty of encounters with ideas that sounded like complete gibberish at first. What changed that was working through the discomfort one component at a time, especially by hand. A definition started to make sense. A step became justified. Eventually, something that had seemed inaccessible became an idea I could actually use.

That feeling of grasping a difficult concept is incredibly rewarding. I wanted to build a tool that makes room for it.

“The times I feel most fulfilled and proud of myself are when I am fully present facing one task.”Griffin Rutherford

AI should help us stay with the work.

The easiest way to use AI on an assignment is often to copy the prompt into ChatGPT or Claude, take the answer, and submit it without evaluating the steps in between. The assignment gets finished. The opportunity to understand it can disappear.

Then I imagine an AI grader assessing that AI-generated submission. There is something funny about the possibility, and something that fills me with existential dread. What is the education system accomplishing if the learner barely needs to participate?

Lune Synth is my response. I want to use applied language models and a robust, gamified interface to bring people back into the process: attempt the problem, inspect the feedback, understand the mistake, and try again.

The friction has a purpose. It gives you decisions to make and reasoning to produce. The interface should help you find the next useful step, while the sense of accomplishment comes from slowly becoming capable of something you could not do before.

Luna + synthesis

A companion. A way of thinking.

Lune comes from Luna, my real poodle and amazing companion. That is her with me in the photo. The name keeps something personal at the heart of the software.

Synth is short for synthesis: developing a deep understanding of a subject and bringing information together coherently. Knowing isolated facts is a beginning. Being able to connect them, explain them, and use them is what I want the learning process to build toward.

A practice habit for whatever you want to learn.

The ambition is a Duolingo-like practice habit for any subject: a test at school, a new language, writing Python, preparing for the MCAT, improving your grammar, and much more.

The gamification should give you a reason to return. What you return to should be meaningful practice: your own steps, useful feedback, and another opportunity to understand. The goal is to leave you with something that lasts beyond the session.

That is the kind of technology I want to spend my time building. And it is the kind of learning I hope Lune Synth helps you experience.

Griffin RutherfordFounder, Lune Synth
